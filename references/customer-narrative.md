# Customer narrative and creative writing

Make the response useful and engaging through its ideas, structure, examples and
language. Write as a friendly, capable partner explaining how the offer supports
the customer's objectives. Creativity changes the expression, not the facts.

## Establish the message before drafting

Identify the buyer's business objectives and evaluation priorities from the tender,
brief and other permitted buyer sources. Keep their source locators in the existing
working record. Distinguish stated objectives from inferred priorities; do not
invent a strategy, numeric target or current customer problem to improve the story.

Form one central proposition: the customer outcome this evidenced offer helps
achieve, and why the proposed approach is credible. For a longer bid, select a
small set of supporting themes and map each to the relevant objective, delivery
mechanism, proof, qualification and sections where it contributes. Use the existing
[working pack](../assets/working-pack.md); a short answer needs only one main idea.
Themes are customer reasons to choose the offer, not internal process labels or
unsupported claims of superiority. Keep approved commercial positioning intact.

## Make every service or process earn its place

For each substantive service/process explanation, connect these ideas naturally:

**What we propose → how it works → what this enables for the customer → which
stated objective it supports → why the evidence makes it credible.**

The customer's 'so what' should name a practical effect: a decision they can make,
a task they can carry out, a risk the control helps manage, or a result the evidence
supports. 'Better service' and 'peace of mind' alone do not explain the mechanism.
Group related steps under one meaningful benefit rather than appending the same
benefit sentence to every technical detail. Preserve the direct answer and any
required technical content.

Qualitative benefits can be supported reasoning from the proposed mechanism;
identify that reasoning privately. They are not measured historic outcomes.
Quantified improvements, causal claims about past results and guarantees need
their own evidence. Do not turn visibility into a promised cost saving, a review
meeting into a new emergency SLA, or a readiness check into zero disruption.
Keep the source's conditions and the distinction between proposal and commitment.

## Write in a friendly, factual voice

Lead with a useful answer and the customer's relevant outcome, then explain the
method and proof. Use clear verbs, familiar words and 'you'/'your team' where the
tender's voice permits. Be warm through clarity, consideration and concrete help;
avoid flattery, exaggerated enthusiasm and claims that you understand feelings or
problems the buyer has not stated. Keep formal declarations and defined terms as
prescribed.

Vary sentence length and openings so the prose reads naturally. Let transitions
explain why the next step matters. Use a short sourced example to make delivery
tangible: relevant setting, actual action, recorded result and relevance here.
Do not invent anecdotes, client quotes, scenes or characters. Remove company-history
preambles, unexplained jargon, inflated adjectives and repeated claims of excellence.
Read the draft aloud or as a busy customer would; revise awkward or impersonal prose.

## Sustain one offer across the document

Each section answers its own question while developing the same customer case:
mobilisation explains how readiness is built; governance explains how unresolved
work becomes visible and actionable; relevant proof explains why the approach is
credible. Adapt that pattern to the actual tender rather than imposing these sections.

Carry the same objectives, terminology, roles, numbers, scope and commitment level
through all answers, summaries and permitted visuals. Reinforce a theme with the
specific contribution of each section, not an identical slogan or repeated case study.
An independently scored answer remains complete without relying on a theme stated
elsewhere. Keep the buyer's required headings; use benefit-led subheadings only
where allowed. Check the opening and closing of adjacent sections for drift.

## Retain the customer benefit under a tight limit

Answer mandatory elements first and fit a specific customer benefit into the
explanation of the method. Cut generic sales introductions before proof, conditions
or the 'so what'. A brief answer can still make a clear customer case. Prescribed
yes/no declarations take their required form; do not insert a sales preamble.

## Example using the fictional company records

Feature-only: 'Our governance framework incorporates weekly client progress
reviews and a shared issue register.'

Customer-focused: 'We propose weekly reviews of one shared issue register, so you
can see the owner and action for each open issue and decide what needs attention
before handover.'

The proposed process is supported by TS-04 v4 in `evals/company/operations/transition-standard.md`.
The decision benefit is reasoned from that mechanism and the fictional buyer's
readiness objective; it is not a measured saving or an authorised new commitment.

Before delivery, check coverage, evidence, customer benefit, consistent message,
natural tone and the exact limits together. Good writing makes supported value
easy to see; it cannot repair an unmet requirement or missing evidence.
